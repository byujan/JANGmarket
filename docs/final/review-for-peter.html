1Summary

HW7 showed JANG reaches more deals than a price-only market, but not better deals. The proposal added four standard negotiation techniques. With rule agents, priority disclosure was the clear win. The Pareto frontier gave the operator a useful view. MESO barely mattered.

When I checked the four changes with Claude agents, the new mandate builder (stage 4) turned out to break deals. Ranking the terms throws away how much each one matters, so many markets ended up with no possible deal before anyone negotiated.

I fixed this in two steps, and both are now in the product. First, the builder asks what each term is worth in money. That brings the deals back, but answer errors let some bad deals through. Second, an optional "Check a few deals" step asks up to three yes/no questions and corrects the weights. That removes the large losses.

A few small losses remain. A safety margin on the walk-away would remove them, but it only pays off when an owner can try several counterparties. So it is written up for a future many-to-many marketplace, not built. The demo stays one-to-one.

2Terms used in this document

Mandate
What the owner signs to authorise their agent: hard limits per term (e.g. price at most $1,300), how much each term matters (weights), the ideal and worst value per term, and the walk-away. JANG refuses any offer outside it.
Utility
How good a package of terms is for the owner, from 0 (every term at its limit) to 1 (every term at its ideal). It is the weighted average of each term's score.
Walk-away (reservation)
The lowest utility at which the agent may still accept. Below it, the owner would rather not trade.
Alternative (outside offer, BATNA)
What the owner will do if this negotiation fails, e.g. "buy the same TV elsewhere for $1,280 with 12 months warranty". Since stage 4, the walk-away is computed as the utility of this alternative, so the owner never types a 0–1 number.
Weights
How much each term matters relative to the others. If the weights are wrong, the agent judges packages wrongly, even with the right limits.
"True" vs "lodged"
In experiments every simulated owner has hidden true preferences. The lodged mandate is what the builder produced from their answers. The gap between the two is the elicitation error.
Rule agent / Claude agent
Rule agents are our deterministic strategy (cheap, used for the big benchmarks). Claude agents are LLMs negotiating through the MCP server (Sonnet 5.5 here). They are realistic but expensive, so the samples are small.
Closing rate
Share of negotiations that ended in a signed deal, out of those where a deal was possible.
Efficiency
Joint gain of the deal (both sides' utility above their walk-aways) as a share of the best joint gain possible in that market. 1.0 is the best possible deal.
Pareto-optimal
No other package is better for one side without being worse for the other.
Below true walk-away
A signed deal that is worse for the owner than their real alternative: the agent should have refused it. This is the safety failure we care about most.
Market cases
Generated TV markets (scripts/hw7/lib.ts). ordinary: random price limits, usually hundreds of dollars of overlap. narrow: the buyer's price ceiling is only $0–50 above the seller's floor. asymmetric: ordinary markets with a much tougher seller agent. nozopa: no deal is possible.

3Where we started: the HW7 submission

The submitted version is main after PR #4 (HW7 benchmark, merged). It compared JANG's four-term negotiation (price, delivery, warranty, bundle) with a price-only negotiation. It used rule agents on generated TV markets (fresh seeds 20261101 and 20261102, 80 markets per case).

ClaimResult (ordinary markets)Verdict
A. JANG reaches deals that price-only can'tclose 81% vs 53%proven
B. JANG's deals are better for both sidesefficiency 0.80 vs 0.84not proven
Narrow marketsclose 18% (price-only 0%)weak

Why claim B failed: our agents never learn what the other side wants. The rule strategy's distanceToTheirAsk weights every term equally, so agents concede on terms the other side doesn't care about and hold firm on the ones it does. Multi-term negotiation should create value by trading what's cheap for you against what's valuable to them. Without knowing what's valuable to them, that trade doesn't happen.

4The four changes from the proposal, and how they did with rule agents

The proposal (docs/final/PROPOSAL.md) took four standard techniques from negotiation teaching. All of them are opt-in, and none touches the scoring core. Each is its own PR, stacked on the previous one.

Stage 1: MESO (multiple equivalent simultaneous offers), PR #5

An agent may attach up to two alternative packages to an offer. All of them must be worth about the same to the agent. The other side picks one, and the pick reveals what it values without either side stating a number. JANG mints an id for every package and checks each one against the proposer's mandate. accept must name the chosen package when there are several. The signed agreement format is unchanged.

Stage 2: priority disclosure, PR #6

The owner can tick a box to share the order of what matters to them (e.g. price > warranty > delivery > bundle), but never the numbers: no weights, no limits, no walk-away. The other agent sees the order and can give ground where it's cheap for itself and valuable to them. Leak tests check that no number from the disclosing mandate reaches the other side.

Stage 3: Pareto frontier in the product, PR #7

The exhaustive search from HW7 moved into the product (src/lib/jang/frontier.ts). For each session JANG computes every feasible package and the frontier. When a deal closes, its efficiency, distance from the frontier and the best missed improvement go into the operator log and an admin chart.

Agents are never told any of this. Telling them "no deal is possible" or "here is a better package" would leak the other side's mandate, and it would enable a probing attack (re-lodge with different limits and binary-search the other side). This is operator-only by decision.

Stage 4: elicitation by choices and the alternative, PR #8

The mandate builder dropped the importance sliders and the "walk away below 0.30" slider. Instead:

  • Which would you rather have? A few questions, each comparing two packages that differ on two terms only. For example: "price at your best but no warranty" vs "price at your limit but 36 months warranty". Binary insertion sorts the terms in at most 5 questions for four terms. A formula (rank-order centroid, ROC) turns the order into weights.
  • If this falls through, what will you do instead? The owner describes their alternative as a package, and the walk-away becomes its utility.

  • worked Disclosure is the first change that moves efficiency. It reaches price-only's 0.84 in ordinary markets and beats it in asymmetric ones (0.85 vs 0.82), while closing more deals too. One-sided disclosure barely helps. "The side that discloses gets exploited" was not observed.
  • worked Pareto frontier: the operator now sees how good each deal was and what was left on the table.
  • little effect MESO adds a little reach and no efficiency, and lowers efficiency in asymmetric markets.
  • Stage 4 changes how owners answer, so it can't be tested with rule agents. That's what the Claude checks were for.

5Checking the four changes with Claude agents

Experiment 1: does the new questionnaire recover what owners want?

16 simulated TV buyers. Each was a Claude instance with hidden true weights and a concrete outside offer, described the way a person knows them ("a week sooner is worth about $X"). Each owner filled in both the old form (sliders) and the new one (choices plus alternative). Each resulting mandate was scored on 4,000 random packages: how often would the agent's accept/refuse call differ from what the owner really wants?

FormOrder of terms rightWrong accept/refuse calls
Old (sliders)0.844.5%
New (choices + alternative)1.00 (perfect)8.1%

Claude answered every new question correctly, so all of the new form's error comes from turning the order into weights. An order says warranty matters more than delivery, but not whether it matters 10% more or 3 times more. ROC guesses fixed ratios, and the walk-away inherits that error because the alternative is valued with the wrong weights.

6Fixing what didn't work, step by step

6.1 Ask what each term is worth in money

After the ranking, the builder asks how much the owner would pay to move each other term from their limit to their best. This is a standard technique (swing weighting).

What the owner sees (TV buyer, price limit $1,300, ideal $999):
"How much is each worth to you? Answer in the same terms as your price. For scale: your whole price range is $301."
• Delivery in 3 days instead of 30: $60
• Warranty 36 months instead of 12: $120
• Soundbar included instead of not: $40

The price range ($301) gets weight 1, and each other term gets its dollar amount divided by $301 (weightsFromSwings). Now the weights carry magnitude: warranty matters twice as much as delivery for this owner, which a ranking can't say. The ranking stays as a consistency check. If the amounts contradict it (say warranty ranked above delivery but given fewer dollars), a short warning shows, and the amounts are used. Answers left blank fall back to the ranking.

Results

  • Simulation with money answers off by about ±30% (no Claude calls): markets left with no possible deal drop from 50% to 31% (narrow) and from 8% to 4% (ordinary).
  • Claude negotiations, same 9 markets: 7/9 closed again, as before the changes.
  • but 2 deals fell below an owner's true walk-away, the first in any arm. One was serious: in asymmetric market #3 the buyer got 0.18 against a walk-away of 0.30, after overstating what the soundbar was worth. When the weights are wrong, the agent believes a bad package is good, and a tough counterparty finds that package.
  • I first wrote that ranking-only weights "fail safe". A simulation showed that's wrong: they allow bad packages just as often (15.6% vs 13.9% of allowed packages). They only looked safe because they closed fewer deals. I corrected this in the memo.

6.2 Guards on the walk-away (dropped)

The idea: accept a package only if it beats the alternative under several plausible versions of the weights, not just the lodged ones. I simulated this over every feasible market (154 ordinary, 57 narrow), 10 draws of answer error each, no Claude calls. Variants:

  • robust ×F: worst case over weights within factor F of the lodged ones
  • top term: never worse than the alternative on the most important term
  • min of two weightings: must win under both the money weights and the ranking weights

All of them trade safety for deals almost one for one. For example, robust ×1.2 cuts bad packages from 13.9% to 3.1% in ordinary markets, but markets with no deal rise from 3.5% to 19%, and most narrow markets die.

6.3 Boundary confirmation (dropped)

Show the owner a few packages near their walk-away and ask whether they'd take them, then move the walk-away to fit the answers. It helps a little (bad packages 13.9% → 11.9%), because moving one number can't fix weights that are wrong in a particular direction. It led directly to 6.4.

6.4 "Check a few deals": correct the weights from yes/no answers (in the product)

The key observation: people find it hard to say what 12 months of warranty is worth in dollars, but easy to look at a concrete deal and say "yes, I'd take that" or "no". This step uses those easy answers to correct the hard ones.

What the owner sees, after entering their alternative:
"Check a few deals. Would you take this instead of your alternative?
Price $1,180 · delivery 10 days · warranty 24 months · no soundbar → Yes / No"
Up to three such questions. It can be skipped.

How it works

  1. Start from the weights the money answers gave. Generate 200 plausible variations around them, each weight randomly scaled by roughly ±30%. Drop any variation that contradicts the owner's ranking.
  2. Pick the package the variations disagree on most: among packages close to the owner's walk-away, the one where about half the variations say "accept" and half say "refuse". The answer to that package is the most informative one possible.
  3. Ask. Each variation that agrees with the answer keeps its weight (×0.95). Each one that disagrees is mostly discounted (×0.05). The model allows for occasional wrong answers rather than trusting any single one completely.
  4. Repeat up to three times. The final weights are the weighted average of the surviving variations, and the walk-away is recomputed from the alternative under those weights.

Code: startRefit, nextCheck, answerCheck, refittedWeights in src/lib/jang/elicit.ts. These are pure functions with a fixed random seed, covered by 5 tests. In the builder, the step only appears once an alternative is entered. If the owner then changes their limits, amounts, ranking or alternative, the old answers no longer apply, and the builder says so.

Results in simulation (no Claude calls, money answers off by about ±30%)

6.5 Getting Claude to use MESO (opt-in)

Since Claude ignored MESO, I added an optional nudge: a short section in the agent prompt (agents/prompts/meso.md), a reminder on each turn, and a hint naming which of our packages the other side's counter came closest to. With the nudge, Claude attached alternatives in 101 of 101 offers. But outcomes got no better: one fewer deal, realised efficiency 0.49 → 0.39, and about twice the cost. So it is off by default (JANG_MESO_GUIDE=on turns it on). It looks like MESO needs a counterparty that reads the choice and adjusts, which Claude doesn't do yet.

6.6 A safety margin on the walk-away (noted, not built)

To remove the remaining small losses, a package must beat the alternative by a margin m (e.g. 0.02), i.e. the walk-away is raised by m.

With one counterparty (simulation)

none (checks only)1.6%8.3%15.1%29.5%

In ordinary markets +0.02 brings bad packages close to the exact-weights level for a few points of no-deal. In narrow markets each +0.01 roughly doubles the no-deal rate, because the zone of possible deals is tiny to begin with.

With several counterparties (simulation)

One-to-one treats a no-deal as a loss. In a marketplace the owner can try someone else, and if nobody works out they take their alternative, which by definition is worth their walk-away. So I simulated one owner meeting up to N counterparties in turn, taking the first deal possible. The same answer error follows them everywhere. "Owner's gain" is true utility minus true walk-away, with no deal = 0.

Narrow marketsCounterpartiesNo dealOwner's avg gainOwner's gain at the edge
no margin114.5%0.024−0.002
no margin55.0%0.028−0.002
+0.02145.1%0.021+0.007
+0.02519.1%0.033+0.012

With one counterparty the margin costs value. From two counterparties on it adds value, because most deals it blocks are found again with the next counterparty, and the deals that happen are better for the owner. More counterparties without a margin don't make anyone safer, since the owner's weight error comes along. The cost is search: about 2.3 counterparties per owner in narrow markets. Since JANG is one-to-one today, the margin is not in the product; it's noted for many-to-many (section 9).

7How the mandate builder works now (the owner's view)

  1. Limits per term: worst acceptable and ideal (e.g. price at most $1,300, ideally $999).
  2. Which would you rather have? 3–5 two-package choices that rank the terms (stage 4).
  3. How much is each worth to you? One dollar amount per non-price term (new, 6.1). Optional; blank falls back to the ranking.
  4. If this falls through: describe the alternative, or tick "no other option" (stage 4).
  5. Check a few deals: up to three yes/no questions that correct the weights (new, 6.4). Optional.
  6. Tell the other side what matters checkbox: disclose the order only (stage 2). Optional.
  7. Sign in the browser. The agent then negotiates under this mandate, and JANG enforces it.

The owner never types a weight or a 0–1 walk-away. Every number in the mandate comes from limits, choices, dollar amounts, an alternative and yes/no answers.

9Decisions made, and what I need from you

Decided

  • The demo stays one-to-one: an owner's agent against the house seller. It is stable, deterministic and tested.
  • Direction after the course: many buyers and many sellers on one negotiation layer. The engine already allows it: any number of agents can lodge mandates for an item, and a session can open between any buyer and seller. Missing pieces:
    • seller listings
    • matching (needs discussion: who meets whom, in what order, by what priority; JANG could connect only pairs whose limits overlap, but being matched leaks a little)
    • an inventory lock (today one TV could be sold twice)
    • views of several negotiations
    Roughly four to seven working days for a demo-quality version.
  • Everything above was designed and tested one-to-one and may change many-to-many. For example:
    • The walk-away becomes "the best other deal on JANG" and moves with the market.
    • Negotiating with several counterparties at once needs rules for commitment and withdrawal.
    • Disclosure and MESO reveal preferences to more parties.
    • Deal quality should be judged against the whole market.
    Written up in docs/limitations.md §5a.
  • The safety margin is noted for many-to-many, not built.
  • The MESO nudge is opt-in only.

What I need from you

  1. Review #5 → #9 in order. #5–#8 touch the core (offers, mandates, stores); #9 is mostly the builder and experiments.
  2. Run the Postgres store and the sign-and-lodge path with the new builder. I couldn't.
  3. Decide what ships for the demo: all four stages, or only disclosure plus the builder fixes. MESO is harmless but adds little.
  4. Your view on the many-to-many direction and on how matching should work.

How much to trust the numbers

Rule-agent results use 58 feasible markets per case. Simulations use 154 ordinary and 57 narrow feasible markets with 10 draws each. Claude negotiations are 9 markets × 1 run per arm, so read them as direction, not proof. Simulated owners answer truthfully, with about ±30% error on dollar amounts. Real people haven't used the builder yet.
